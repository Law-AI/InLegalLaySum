The Calcutta High Court on Monday rejected a public interest lawsuit (PIL). This lawsuit asked the court to order that only vaccinated students in Class 8 should be allowed to attend in-person classes. It also requested that children under 15 years old (those born after 2007) be allowed to take online classes instead.  A lawyer named Gaurav Purakayastha, who lives in the Birbhum district, had filed this lawsuit.  It's important to know that the central government had previously announced that children aged 15 or older (born in 2007 or earlier) could get the COVID-19 vaccine starting January 3, 2022.  Two judges, Chief Justice Prakash Shrivastava and Justice Rajarshi Bharadwaj, dismissed the request. They said there was a "serious question about whether the person who filed the lawsuit had the right to do so" (meaning his 'locus'), and they also noted that the lawsuit didn't have enough evidence or documents to support it.  The judges also thought that since another similar lawsuit about schools reopening was already in court, taking on this new one would just create too many court cases about the same thing. However, they allowed the lawyer to file a new request later, if he had all the necessary evidence.  Notably, on January 28, 2022, the High Court had asked the state government to respond to several other lawsuits about reopening schools and colleges in the state. After that, the state government announced that students from Class 8 and above could return to in-person classes, and colleges could also reopen.  **Submissions on behalf of the petitioner**  The lawyer representing Gaurav Purakayastha, Ritzu Ghosai, argued that children born after 2007 haven't been vaccinated because they aren't covered by the central government's vaccine rules. This means they are more likely to get infected with COVID-19.  He also claimed that these unvaccinated students were being forced to attend classes in person. To support this, he showed a notice from a private school, DPS Megacity in Kolkata. This notice said that all Class 8 students *had* to attend in-person classes starting February 7, 2022. The notice also mentioned that students would be split into two groups, with each group attending physical classes on alternating days.  **Submissions on behalf of the State government**  The Advocate General, S.N. Mookherjee, who represented the state government, strongly disagreed with the lawsuit. He questioned whether the person who filed it had the legal standing to do so (his 'locus standi'). He pointed out that the lawyer was not a parent of a student, nor was he an educator working in a school. The court was also told that the lawyer lived in Birbhum district but was bringing up an issue about schools reopening in Kolkata.  He further argued that the lawsuit lacked proper evidence. He said it was based only on a notice from a single private school, which made the entire lawsuit invalid in the eyes of the law.  It was also argued that if students were separated into vaccinated and unvaccinated groups, and only vaccinated students could attend in person, it would be unfair to the others. The Advocate General also suggested that if the court granted the lawyer's request, it would create a 'digital divide,' which means some students would have access to technology and others wouldn't, and this would be harmful. He informed the court that holding online classes in government schools was almost impossible because children in rural areas often don't have internet access.  He also stated that the state government had already made a formal decision to reopen schools and had issued an announcement about it on January 31, 2022.  He also referred to a report from the World Health Organization (WHO) that described the harmful social and economic effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on children. Because of this, he argued that children were suffering due to schools being closed.  The court was also told that the number of positive COVID-19 tests in the state had dropped significantly to 3.3 percent, showing a sharp decrease in cases. All schools had been told to follow COVID-19 safety rules. The Advocate General added that if any school failed to follow these rules, proper complaints could be filed.  It was also mentioned that many other states had allowed schools to reopen. In Delhi, schools had reopened that very day, even though its COVID-19 positive test rate and case numbers were higher than in West Bengal.  The state government also referred to a decision by the Madhya Pradesh High Court in a case called Aditya Singh Solanki v. State of Madhya Pradesh. In that case, a similar public interest lawsuit had been rejected because the court believed it was filed just to get attention.  **Submissions on behalf of the Union of India**  The lawyer representing the national government, Y.J. Dastoor, argued that the central government had been included in the lawsuit without need, as no specific requests were made against it. However, he told the court that the national government supported the state government's decision to reopen schools and other learning institutions.
