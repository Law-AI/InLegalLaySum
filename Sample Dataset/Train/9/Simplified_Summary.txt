Someone asked the court to enforce a previous court order (a decree). This request was made more than 12 years after the original court order and 3 years after the last attempt to enforce it.  The person who wanted to enforce the order (the decree holder) said that the person who owed the money (the judgment debtor) had unfairly bought a business using someone else's name. They claimed this was done to hide the business's money, so it couldn't be used to pay the debt. Because of this, the decree holder argued that they should be allowed to enforce the order, even after 19 years, according to Section 48 of the Civil Procedure Code.  The High Court decided that because the judgment debtor's fraud prevented the decree holder from enforcing the order, Section 48 didn't block the request. However, the court also said that because the request was made more than 3 years after the last attempt to enforce the order, it was blocked by Article 182 of the Limitation Act (a law that sets time limits for legal actions).  The decree holder appealed to the Supreme Court. They argued that because fraud was proven for Section 48 of the Civil Procedure Code, Section 18 of the Limitation Act should apply. This would mean the request wasn't blocked because it was made within three years of discovering the fraud. They also argued that Article 181 should be used instead. The Supreme Court decided: (i) Whether Section 18 applies is a legal question that can be raised even if it wasn't brought up in lower courts. (ii) Even though Section 48 and Articles 181 and 189 deal with time limits for enforcing court orders and should be considered together, they have different purposes. Just because Section 48 doesn't block the request doesn't mean Article 182 doesn't apply. (iii) The fraud didn't hide the right to enforce the order; it only made it harder to collect from a specific business. So, Section 18 doesn't apply, and the request is blocked by Article 182. (iv) Even though Article 182 doesn't mention fraud, it still applies because it should be interpreted with Section 18, which covers fraud.  The court also said: (i) A court order that requires someone to pay court fees before enforcing it isn't conditional. The time limit for enforcing it starts from the date of the order, not when the fees are paid.  (ii) The time spent trying to declare the judgment debtor bankrupt can't be excluded when calculating the time limit for enforcing the order, according to Section 14(2) of the Limitation Act.  The Supreme Court agreed with the Bombay High Court's decision.
