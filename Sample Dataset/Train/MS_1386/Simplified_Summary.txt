The Karnataka High Court has ruled that a lender who holds property as security for a loan (called a secured creditor) has the first right to claim that property. This rule comes from a law known as the SARFAESI Act. The Court also said that laws protecting tenants (Rent Act) do not overrule the SARFAESI Act.  A panel of two judges, Chief Justice Ritu Raj Awasthi and Justice Sachin Shankar Magadum, stated that a secured lender, who has a legal claim on a property, gets paid first when that property is sold, before any other claims.  The person who appealed the case, Abdul Khader, was a tenant in a property that was used as collateral for a loan from the Bank of Maharashtra. Khader challenged an earlier decision by a single judge. That decision had told him to use a different legal process under Section 17 of the SARFAESI Act, before a special court (tribunal).  Khader told the Court, using a specific part of the SARFAESI Act (Section 14(3)), that he had no way to appeal the decision. Because of this, he believed he was right to ask the High Court for help directly. He also claimed the single judge had not considered this point.  He also argued, using another law called the Transfer of Property Act (Section 65-A), that if a property used as collateral is rented out with a valid lease, local officials like a Magistrate cannot use the SARFAESI Act (Section 14) to take control of it.  Khader further claimed that if a tenant or borrower is living in a property used as collateral, the tenant should be protected from unfair evictions under the Rent Control Act.  Court findings:  First, the court looked at whether someone affected by the bank's actions, but not the original borrower, could challenge a Magistrate's decision under the SARFAESI Act (Section 14). The court pointed to Section 17(4A) of the SARFAESI Act. It said this part of the law clearly shows that a tenant, like Khader, who claims renting rights to the property, has an effective way to challenge the bank's actions through Section 17(4A) of the SARFAESI Act.  The court also explained that the SARFAESI Act (and another law, the DRT Act) not only helps banks quickly get back money they are owed. It also offers proper legal ways for people living in the collateral property, whether as tenants or with other rights, to get help.  Next, the court looked at its second main question: whether tenant protection laws (Rent Act) are more powerful than the SARFAESI Act.  The court based its decision on Section 26-E of the SARFAESI Act. This section clearly states that, even if other laws say something different, once a lender's claim on a property is officially recorded, the money owed to that lender must be paid before all other debts. This includes even taxes or fees owed to the government.  The court then explained that the special phrase in Section 26-E, which means "despite any other law," clearly shows that lawmakers intended for secured lenders to always have the highest claim. This means they get paid first, even over other legal claims. They also have the first right to sell the property to recover their money. This phrase makes Section 26-E more powerful than any other law.  The court also noted that the rental agreement was made *after* the property owner had already given the property's legal papers to the bank as collateral for a loan in November 2017. Because of this, the court said it didn't understand how Khader could claim rights under the Transfer of Property Act (Section 65-A) through his lease. Even if he did have such rights, the court repeated that under Section 26-E of the SARFAESI Act, the secured lender still gets paid first. Therefore, the bank had every right to take action against the property.  For all these reasons, the court rejected Khader's appeal, stating that his arguments had no legal value.
